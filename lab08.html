What a network administrator needs to know about VPNs

A VPN carries private traffic across a network you don't control. The administrator's job runs the full life cycle: plan it, configure it, prove it works, and keep it working. Read this briefing, then complete Parts A and B.

Site-to-site

Two gateways (routers or firewalls) protect whole networks. Users never run VPN software. Part A builds this with IPsec in tunnel mode.

Point-to-point

A one-to-one tunnel between exactly two endpoints, such as a home-office laptop and an office server. Part B builds this with WireGuard.

Remote access

Many individual users connect to one concentrator with a client app (IKEv2, SSL/TLS, OpenVPN). Covered in this briefing and the knowledge check.

Knowledge an administrator needs

Concepts

  • VPN topologies: site-to-site, remote access (client-to-site), point-to-point (host-to-host), hub-and-spoke, full mesh, and dynamic multipoint (DMVPN).
  • Tunnel vs transport mode: tunnel mode wraps the entire original packet in a new IP header (gateway to gateway). Transport mode protects only the payload and keeps the original header (host to host, or GRE over IPsec).
  • Cryptography basics: symmetric ciphers (AES-GCM, AES-CBC, ChaCha20), integrity (HMAC-SHA-256/384), key exchange (Diffie-Hellman groups 14, 19, 20, 21), authentication (pre-shared keys vs. certificates/PKI).
  • IPsec architecture: IKE phase 1 (IKE SA, management channel) and phase 2 (IPsec/child SAs that carry data), security parameter index (SPI), lifetimes and rekeying, perfect forward secrecy (PFS).
  • Policy-based vs route-based VPNs: crypto ACLs ("interesting traffic") vs. virtual tunnel interfaces (VTI) with routing.
  • Split vs full tunneling and their security and bandwidth trade-offs.

Supporting network knowledge

  • IP addressing: non-overlapping private subnets at every site, wildcard masks, CIDR.
  • NAT and NAT traversal: NAT exemption for VPN traffic, NAT-T over UDP 4500, why AH breaks behind NAT.
  • Firewalls and ports: UDP 500/4500, ESP (IP 50), WireGuard UDP 51820, TLS 443.
  • MTU and MSS: encryption overhead (50 to 80 bytes) causes fragmentation; adjust TCP MSS to around 1360.
  • Identity: AAA, RADIUS (UDP 1812/1813), LDAP/Active Directory, SAML, multifactor authentication.
  • Supporting services: DNS (split DNS for internal names), NTP (certificate validation and log timestamps), syslog/SIEM.
Skills an administrator must demonstrate

Plan

  • Gather requirements: who connects, to what, how many users, required throughput and uptime.
  • Choose the VPN type, protocol, and crypto suite that meet security policy and device support.
  • Design addressing, routing, and which traffic is protected (split or full tunnel).
  • Plan authentication, key and certificate management, and firewall/NAT changes.

Configure

  • Build IKE/IPsec policies, keys, transform sets, crypto ACLs or tunnel interfaces from the CLI.
  • Configure VPN on SOHO routers and firewalls through a web GUI.
  • Generate and distribute keys (WireGuard), certificates (PKI), and client profiles.
  • Open the right firewall ports and create NAT exemptions.

Test and troubleshoot

  • Verify underlay reachability first, then the tunnel.
  • Read SA tables (show crypto isakmp sa, wg show) and packet counters.
  • Use debugs and packet captures to isolate phase 1, phase 2, routing, or firewall problems.

Maintain

  • Rotate keys and renew certificates before expiration.
  • Patch VPN firmware promptly; VPN gateways are a top target for attackers.
  • Monitor tunnel state, logs, capacity, and failed logins; review access regularly.
  • Document configurations, addressing, and change history.

VPN life cycle: plan, configure, verify, maintain
  1. Gather requirements. Sites, users, applications, bandwidth, uptime, and compliance rules.
  2. Design addressing and topology. Confirm subnets don't overlap; pick site-to-site, remote access, or point-to-point.
  3. Select protocols and crypto. For example IKEv2, AES-256-GCM, SHA-256, DH group 14 or higher, PFS.
  4. Plan authentication. Pre-shared keys for labs and small sites; certificates and MFA for production.
  5. Prepare the edge. Allow UDP 500/4500 and ESP (or UDP 51820), add NAT exemptions, reserve static public IPs or dynamic DNS.
  6. Configure both ends with mirror-image parameters.
  7. Verify. Test underlay first, generate interesting traffic, check SAs, counters, and application access.
  8. Document and hand off. Diagrams, parameters (never the keys themselves), and a test record.
  9. Monitor and maintain. Log review, rekey and certificate renewals, firmware patches, capacity checks.

Inside a WireGuard packet

The laptop's ping to 10.10.0.1 after it leaves the home router. NAT rewrote the outer source address; the inner packet is untouched and unreadable.

Outer IP198.51.100.77 to 203.0.113.10
UDP41824 to 51820
WG headertype 4, receiver index, counter
Inner IP10.10.0.2 to 10.10.0.1
ICMPecho
Data
Poly1305 tag16 bytes

Like IPsec tunnel mode, WireGuard hides the inner addresses. Unlike IPsec, it always uses UDP, which passes through NAT easily, and it has no cipher negotiation: every peer uses ChaCha20-Poly1305, so there's nothing to mismatch except keys, addresses, and ports.
